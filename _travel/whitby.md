Whitby sits on the North Yorkshire coast, where the River Esk meets the North Sea. The abbey
ruins stand on the East Cliff above the town, at the top of the 199 steps. King Oswiu of
Northumbria founded the first monastery here in 657, and St Hild ran it as abbess. In 664
the Synod of Whitby met here and chose the Roman date for Easter over the Irish one. Cædmon,
the earliest English poet we know by name, kept the cattle at this monastery. Viking raids
destroyed it in the ninth century. Benedictine monks refounded the abbey after the Norman
Conquest, and the Gothic church you see today went up from about 1220. Henry VIII dissoluted
the abbey in 1539, and the church has stood open to the sky ever since. German warships
shelled it in December 1914. Bram Stoker stayed in Whitby in 1890 and found the name
"Dracula" in a book in the public library. In his novel, the Russian ship Demeter runs
aground in the harbour during a storm with its crew dead. Dracula leaps ashore as a large
black dog and runs up the 199 steps to the churchyard of St Mary's, below the abbey. Stoker
took the wreck from a real one, the Dmitry from Narva, which ran aground here in 1885.
