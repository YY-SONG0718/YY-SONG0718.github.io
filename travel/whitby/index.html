Whitby

A walk up to the abbey ruins on the cliff above the harbour, and the town that gave Dracula his landing place.

Whitby sits on the North Yorkshire coast, where the River Esk meets the North Sea. The abbey ruins stand on the East Cliff above the town, at the top of the 199 steps. King Oswiu of Northumbria founded the first monastery here in 657, and St Hild ran it as abbess. In 664 the Synod of Whitby met here and chose the Roman date for Easter over the Irish one. Cædmon, the earliest English poet we know by name, kept the cattle at this monastery. Viking raids destroyed it in the ninth century. Benedictine monks refounded the abbey after the Norman Conquest, and the Gothic church you see today went up from about 1220. Henry VIII dissoluted the abbey in 1539, and the church has stood open to the sky ever since. German warships shelled it in December 1914. Bram Stoker stayed in Whitby in 1890 and found the name “Dracula” in a book in the public library. In his novel, the Russian ship Demeter runs aground in the harbour during a storm with its crew dead. Dracula leaps ashore as a large black dog and runs up the 199 steps to the churchyard of St Mary’s, below the abbey. Stoker took the wreck from a real one, the Dmitry from Narva, which ran aground here in 1885.

The abbey

The abbey church from the south lawn. The presbytery stands at the left, the nave at the right.
The same ruin in two kinds of light. The cloud moved fast off the sea and changed the stone from gold to grey within minutes.
The east front, with three tiers of lancet windows. The beach at Sandsend lies in the distance to the right.
Inside the nave. The arcade, the triforium and the clerestory still stand in three storeys, with nothing above them but sky.

The harbour

The harbour and the West Cliff, seen from the old town on the east side. The Royal Crescent and the spire of St Hilda's church mark the skyline.
